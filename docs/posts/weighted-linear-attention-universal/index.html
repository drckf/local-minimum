TLDR

This post generalizes the results in my previous posts about weighted linear attention to show that the connections between weighted linear attention and ecological dynamics hold for any loss function satisfying some mild conditions described below (see Assumption 1). The previous posts in this series are:

  • What is Weighted Linear Attention?
  • Weighted Linear Attention is Lotka-Volterra Dynamics
  • Weighted Linear Attention with Other Loss Functions
  • Weighted Linear Attention as Species Invasion
  • Weighted Linear Attention with Mahalanobis Distance

As an aside, or perhaps as a warning, I’ve tried to write this post in the “theorem-proof” format that is typical of machine learning publications even though, as a physicist, I find it both unnatural and a bit unnecessary. But, sometimes it is good to try new things.

Main result

Let \(\vec{q}_l\), \(\vec{k}_l\), and \(\vec{v}_l\) for \(l = 1, \ldots, L\) denote the query, key, and value vectors of dimension \(d_k\), \(d_k\), and \(d_v\), respectively. And, let \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) be the associated query, key, and value matrices of dimension \(d_k \times L\), \(d_k \times L\), and \(d_v \times L\), respectively. Define the linear attention matrix as \[ J( \vec{w} ) = \sum_{l=1}^L w_l \, \vec{v}_l \, \vec{k}_l^T, \] with linear recall as \(\tilde{v}_l = J(\vec{w}) \, \vec{q}_l\). Our goal is to choose the weights \(\vec{w} \geq 0\) in order to minimize some differentiable loss function, \(\mathcal{L}\). While training a weighted linear attention module \(J( \vec{w} )\) to minimize a differentiable loss function \(\mathcal{L}\) using unnormalized exponentiated gradient descent, the dynamics of the weights are described by a generalized Lotka-Volterra equation \[ \frac{d w_l}{d t} = w_l \left( s_l - \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l'} A_{l,l'}(\vec{w}) \right) \,, \] where the intrinsic growth rate and interaction coefficients are \[ \begin{align} s_l &= \vec{k}_l^T \, \Sigma_{qv} \, \vec{v_l} \, , \\ A_{l,l'}(\vec{w}) &= \vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_{l'} \, \left( \sum_{j} \vec{v}_l^T \, \, F_{l',j}(\vec{w}) \, \vec{v}_j \right) \,, \end{align} \] with \(L \times L\) matrix \[ F^T(\vec w) = V^{\dagger} \bigl (L \, G + V \bigr) \] where \(V^{\dagger}\) denotes the Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse of \(V\) and \(G\) is a \(d_v \times L\) matrix with columns \(\partial \mathcal{L} / \partial \tilde{v}_j\).

Theory

Definition 1: Weighted Linear Attention

Let \(\vec{q}_l\), \(\vec{k}_l\), and \(\vec{v}_l\) for \(l = 1, \ldots, L\) denote the query, key, and value vectors of dimension \(d_k\), \(d_k\), and \(d_v\), respectively. And, let \(Q\), \(K\), and \(V\) be the associated query, key, and value matrices of dimension \(d_k \times L\), \(d_k \times L\), and \(d_v \times L\), respectively. Define the \(d_v \times d_k\) linear attention matrix as \[ J( \vec{w} ) = \sum_{l=1}^L w_l \, \vec{v}_l \, \vec{k}_l^T, \] with linear recall via \(\tilde{v}_l = J(\vec{w}) \, \vec{q}_l\). Typically, one aims to learn the weights by minimizing some differentiable loss function \(\mathcal{L}(\{\tilde{v}_l, \vec{v}_l \}_{l=1}^L)\) that depends on the observed and recollected value vectors.

Lemma 1: Gradients of Weighted Linear Attention

Let \(\mathcal{L}\) be a differentiable loss function depending on the \(\tilde{v}_i\) and define \[ \vec{g}_i := \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \tilde{v}_i} \in \mathbb{R}^{d_v}. \] Then the derivative of \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to the weight \(w_l\) is given by \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = \sum_{i=1}^L \left( \vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i \right) \left( \vec{g}_i^T \, \vec{v}_l \right). \]

Proof

The proof is simply an application of the chain rule of calculus. We begin with the definition of the predicted output, \(\tilde{v}_i = \sum_{j=1}^L w_j \, \vec{v}_j (\vec{k}_j^T \, \vec{q}_i)\). Since \(\mathcal{L}\) depends on \(\vec{w}\) only through the \(\tilde{v}_i\), the chain rule yields \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = \sum_{i=1}^L \left\langle \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial \tilde{v}_i}, \frac{\partial \tilde{v}_i}{\partial w_l} \right\rangle. \] Note that differentiating \(\tilde{v}_i\) with respect to \(w_l\) gives \(\frac{\partial \tilde{v}_i}{\partial w_l} = \vec{v}_l (\vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i)\). Thus, \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = \sum_{i=1}^L \Bigl\langle \vec{g}_i \, \vec{v}_l (\vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i) \Bigr\rangle = \sum_{i=1}^L (\vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i) (\vec{g}_i^T \, \vec{v}_l). \] QED.

Assumption 1: Gradient Decomposition

Let
\[ V = \bigl[ \vec{v}_1 \, \dots \, \vec{v}_L \bigr] \in \mathbb R^{d_v \times L}, \qquad G = \bigl[ \vec{g}_1 \, \dots\ , \vec{g}_L \bigr] \in \mathbb R^{d_v \times L}. \] We assume that for every parameter vector \(\vec{w}\) there exists a matrix \(F(\vec{w}) \in \mathbb R^{L\times L}\) such that
\[ L \, G \;=\; -\, V \;+\; V \, F^T \, \tag{A1} \] or, equivalently, \[ \vec{g}_i \;=\; -\frac{1}{L} \, \vec{v}_i \;+\; \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^{L} F_{ij}(\vec{w}) \, \vec{v}_j, \qquad i=1,\dots ,L. \] This holds iff each gradient \(\vec{g}_i = \partial \mathcal{L}/ \partial \tilde{v}_i\) lies in the linear span of the value vectors \(\{ \vec{v}_j \}\), as is the case for quadratic and cross-entropy losses containing the term \(\sum_i \tilde{v}_i^{\!\top} \vec{v}_i\). If a left Moore–Penrose pseudoinverse \(V^{\dagger}\) exists (e.g. \(\operatorname{rank} V = L\)), the minimum-norm coefficient matrix is
\[ F^T(\vec w) = V^{\dagger} \bigl( L \, G + V \bigr) \, . \tag{A2} \] When \(V\) is rank-deficient, other solutions exist, but (A2) gives the canonical choice.

Lemma 2: Continuous-time Unnormalized Exponentiated Gradient Descent

Let \(\mathcal{L}(\vec{w})\) be a differentiable loss function of non-negative weights \(\vec{w} = (w_1, \dots, w_L)\). Apply the unnormalized exponentiated-gradient update \[ w_l^{\;'} = w_l \, e^{-\eta \, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l}}, \qquad l = 1, \dots, L, \] with step size \(\eta > 0\). In the limit \(\eta \to 0\) (continuous-time), the weight trajectory \(w_l(t)\) satisfies the differential equation \[ \frac{d w_l}{d t} = - w_l \, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l}. \]

Proof
Consider a single coordinate \(w_l\) and apply the update rule: \[ w_l(t + \eta) = w_l(t) \, e^{-\eta \, \partial_{w_l} \mathcal{L}(\vec{w}(t))}. \] Taking logarithms of both sides: \[ \ln w_l(t + \eta) - \ln w_l(t) = -\eta \, \partial_{w_l} \mathcal{L}(\vec{w}(t)). \] Dividing both sides by \(\eta\) and taking the limit \(\eta \to 0\): \[ \lim_{\eta \to 0} \frac{\ln w_l(t + \eta) - \ln w_l(t)}{\eta} = - \partial_{w_l} \mathcal{L}(\vec{w}(t)). \] The left-hand side is the time derivative of \(\ln w_l(t)\), which gives: \[ \frac{1}{w_l} \frac{d w_l}{d t} = - \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l}. \] Multiplying both sides by \(w_l\) yields the result: \[ \frac{d w_l}{d t} = - w_l \, \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l}. \] QED.

Theorem 1: Linear Attention via Lotka-Volterra Dynamics

Define the empirical query–value covariance matrix as \[ \Sigma_{qv} = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{v}_i^T. \] Then, according to the Gradient Decomposition Assumption, there exists a function \(F_{ij}(\vec{w})\) defined by \[ F^T(\vec w) = V^{\dagger} \bigl( L \, G + V \bigr) \, . \tag{A2} \] such that the gradient of the loss function \(\mathcal{L}\) with respect to weight \(w_l\) can be expressed as \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = - \left( s_l - \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l'=1}^L A_{l,l'}(\vec{w}) \right) \] where \[ \begin{align} s_l &= \vec{k}_l^T \, \Sigma_{qv} \, \vec{v_l} \, , \\ A_{l,l'}(\vec{w}) &= \vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_{l'} \, \left( \sum_{j} \vec{v}_l^T \, \, F_{l',j}(\vec{w}) \, \vec{v}_j \right) \,. \end{align} \] Moreover, the dynamics of the weights under unnormalized exponentiated gradient descent are described by the generalized Lotka-Volterra equation \[ \frac{d w_l}{d t} = w_l \left( s_l - \frac{1}{L} \sum_{l'} A_{l,l'}(\vec{w}) \right) \,. \]

Proof

Recall the expression for the gradient from Lemma 1: \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = \sum_{i=1}^L \left( \vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i \right)\left( \vec{g}_i^T \, \vec{v}_l \right). \] We now aim to express this in the form \[ \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} = - \vec{k}_l^T \, \Sigma_{qv} \, \vec{v}_l + \vec{k}_l^T \, M \, \vec{v}_l = - \vec{k}_l^T \, \left( \Sigma_{qv} - M \right) \, \vec{v}_l, \] where \[ M = \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^L \sum_{j=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, F_{ij}(\vec{w})\, \vec{v}_j^T. \] From the Gradient Decomposition Assumption, we assume that \[ \vec{g}_i = - \frac{1}{L} \vec{v}_i + \frac{1}{L} \sum_{j=1}^L F_{ij}(\vec{w}) \vec{v}_j. \] Notice that although the gradient formula from Lemma 1 involves a positive sum of dot products, the resulting formula in the claimed theorem has an overall negative sign that results from the negative leading term in the gradient decomposition assumption. Multiplying both sides on the left by \(\vec{q}_i\) and summing over \(i\), we find \[ \begin{align} \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{g}_i^T &= -\frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{v}_i^T + \frac{1}{L} \sum_{i=1}^L \sum_{j=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, F_{ij}(\vec{w}) \, \vec{v}_j^T \\ &= -\Sigma_{qv} + M. \end{align} \] Rearranging gives \[ M = \Sigma_{qv} + \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{g}_i^T. \] Now substitute this into the expression for the gradient: \[ \begin{align} \frac{\partial \mathcal{L}}{\partial w_l} &= - \vec{k}_l^T \, \left( \Sigma_{qv} - M \right) \, \vec{v}_l \\ &= - \vec{k}_l^T \, \left( \Sigma_{qv} - \left[\Sigma_{qv} + \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{g}_i^T \right] \right) \, \vec{v}_l \\ &= \vec{k}_l^T \, \left( \sum_{i=1}^L \vec{q}_i \, \vec{g}_i^T \right) \, \vec{v}_l \\ &= \sum_{i=1}^L (\vec{k}_l^T \, \vec{q}_i)(\vec{g}_i^T \, \vec{v}_l), \end{align} \] which recovers the direct chain-rule result. Given equivalence of these two forms for the gradients of the loss function, the fact that the dynamics under exponentiated gradient descent are described by the claimed generalized Lotka-Volterra equations follows immediately from Lemma 2.

QED.
